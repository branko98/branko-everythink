## Where this sits

That isn’t a CRM problem. A B2B buyer moves across channels, devices and months, and every tool in the stack sees one slice of that. Each one is right on its own terms. **Nobody had decided which one was official for which question.**

This is the reporting and AI layer built on top of the measurement rebuild described in the [main growth case study](/case-studies/tapi/#revenue-measurement-and-attribution). Same engagement, built once the diagnosis was done and the first fixes were holding. No new platform, and nothing moved out of the systems it already lived in.

## What was actually wrong

<Lede>None of this is unusual. It’s what a reporting stack looks like after a few years of tools being added one at a time, each for a good reason.</Lede>

- **Every system told a different story.** <Flag>Ad platforms and the CRM disagreed by 15–20%</Flag>, and nobody could say which side was right, or why.
- **Two thirds of new customers had no marketing channel at all.** Contacts from an old CRM migration, phone calls and event lists all entered as Offline.
- **The monthly report took three working days**, and <Flag>four versions of the same number were in circulation</Flag> while it was being assembled.
- **Failures were silent.** The in-app browser form failure in the main case study was found by working down four layers by hand. Nothing in the stack would have flagged it.

## Three rules, then the build

<Lede>The architecture follows from three decisions. Every one of them is about authority and risk, not about tooling - which is why the tools stayed where they were.</Lede>

**One official source for every metric.** MRR comes from billing. Channel comes from the first conversion in the CRM, frozen at that point so a later touch can’t overwrite it. A second system cross-checks each one, and there’s a defined outcome when they conflict.

**The agent flags, people decide.** Nothing is written back to the CRM automatically. A correction is a human action with a reason attached to it.

**Estimates stay separate from facts.** View-through is reported as a channel-level estimate and never attached to an individual customer, because at the level of a person it isn’t knowable.

## How it was built

**The foundation came first.** Two separated measurement layers, a lead capture path on a first-party domain with a Worker recording every submission before it posts, and lifecycle stages flowing back to the ad platforms as offline conversions. That work is the subject of the [main case study](/case-studies/tapi/) and everything below depends on it - **a reporting layer over unreliable inputs is a faster way to be confidently wrong**.

**Then the layer on top.** The warehouse joins ad spend, the CRM and billing. The ChatGPT Ads test has no connector, so those results are imported weekly rather than inferred into the pipeline. A small internal app logs every manual correction with a reason, writes factual fixes back to the CRM once a person approves them, and locks the monthly report once it’s closed.

**And one piece of judgement work that isn’t arithmetic.** Free-text answers to *how did you hear about us* get sorted into channels by model, which gives a third independent view alongside click attribution and CRM channel. It’s the one place in the system where a model decides anything, and it’s there because the input is language and nothing else can read it.

**Build versus buy.** Off-the-shelf attribution platforms were evaluated before any of this. None of them would have answered the question that caused the problem: which system is official when two disagree, and what happens to the record when they do. **That is a decision a company makes once, not a feature a licence supplies.** The build also keeps the first-party data portable into whatever gets built on it next.

## What the agent is allowed to do

<Lede>The agent is the nightly checks and the alerts they raise. It reads, compares and reports. It does not write.</Lede>

Almost all of it is deterministic - rules written once and run on a schedule. A model is involved in one place only, where the input is language. Arithmetic doesn’t need judgement, and judgement shouldn’t be applied to arithmetic.

That sounds like a limitation and it’s the opposite. An automation with write access to the CRM is a system where <Flag>a wrong inference becomes a wrong record</Flag>, and the person who finds it six weeks later has no way to tell which ones were machine-made. An automation that only flags leaves every change traceable to a person and a reason.

**The cost of being wrong is what decides the boundary.** A bad alert in Slack costs someone ninety seconds. A bad write to a customer record costs a quarter of reporting.

<CorrectionLoop {...fig.correctionLoop} />

Eight checks run every night. After tuning, roughly one alert in eight is a false positive; early on it was closer to one in three. That rate is worth stating rather than hiding: a check tuned never to cry wolf is a check that misses things, and **which way to err is a decision, not an accident**.

## What changed

<Lede>Most of the improvement came from the first step - fixing the inputs.</Lede>

The layer on top brought something harder to put a number on: gaps are now explained rather than argued about, and **a failure surfaces in a day or two instead of at the end of a quarter**.

### And what it changed about decisions.

**Budget moved on one number.** Cost per property entering pipeline is read from the same locked report every month. The audience reallocation in the main case study rested on figures nobody disputed - which is the only reason it survived contact with the people whose plan it overturned.

**A channel was cut in one market on evidence, not on patience.** LinkedIn in Australia showed no contribution at any pipeline stage within weeks, which matched the structural read - the profession doesn’t gather on that network in that market. The budget moved to the UK instead of waiting out a quarter of argument.

**Events were measured by outcome for the first time.** Contacts from conferences are now tied to the deals they become, which decides which ones stay in next year’s plan.

## What this generalises to

**The problem was never a bad CRM.** It was the absence of a rule about which system is official for which metric. That rule costs nothing to write and is the thing almost nobody writes.

**The model is useful where judgement is needed, not where arithmetic is.** The system calculates. The model reads language - free-text answers, call transcripts - and a person approves anything that leaves the building.

**Manual corrections are fine as long as they leave a trail.** The trail is what turns a repeated correction from an annoyance into a defect report against the source.

**One limitation stands.** View-through and word-of-mouth without a link can’t be seen at the level of a person, so they’re reported as estimates and nothing in this system pretends otherwise.
